The first family at the south gate would not open their door.

An old man shouted through the crack that the Bureau of Winds had already posted its notice: clear skies tomorrow. Why should anyone carry their rice jars out into the dark?

Gu Zhiwei did not argue about the weather. She held the forecast up to his lantern and turned it over. On the back were the evacuation routes, and beside them, the Bureau's seal.

“The front says clear skies,” she said. “The back says move tonight. Both are stamped. Which one do you want to bet your house on?”

The old man stared at the red seal for a long time. Then he went back inside and woke his grandson.

After that, it went faster. The chief official walked at her side with a lantern, saying nothing, and knocked on doors himself. Gu Zhiwei sent the lowest lanes first, the ones she had marked lowest on the back of the forecast, and kept the families on higher ground for last. Every so often she stopped to look at the river. The water had turned the color of strong tea.

Near midnight, a runner from the governor's residence caught up with them. The governor had heard that the Bureau was moving people out on the eve of his wedding. He wanted to know who had given the order.

The chief official took the forecast from Gu Zhiwei, folded it so the back faced outward, and handed it to the runner.

“Tell the governor the Bureau of Winds gave it,” he said. “The seal is mine.”

By the fourth watch, more than four hundred families were crowded into the courtyard of the City God Temple. Children slept on bundles of bedding. Someone had lit a fire under the eaves.

Gu Zhiwei could not sleep. She walked around the main hall and found, on a pillar behind the incense table, a column of scratches worn smooth by years of hands. They were numbers—rising, falling, rising again—each with a date beside it.

It took her a moment to understand. They were air pressure readings, taken by hand, thirty years old.

The last line was not a number. In the same handwriting, with the last stroke of *water* flicked upward, it read:

**I watched the water from here until the morning. No one came.**

Gu Zhiwei pressed her palm against the pillar. Behind her, the courtyard was full of people breathing in their sleep.

At dawn the sky over the city was perfectly clear. Some of the families began to grumble and talk about going home.

Gu Zhiwei looked to the southwest. Above the mountains, a thin line of cloud had begun to thicken at its base.

“Not yet,” she said. “It hasn't reached late afternoon.”
