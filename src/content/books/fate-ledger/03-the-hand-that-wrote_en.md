The lamp went out as the fifth stroke rolled down the mountain.

In the dark, the Hall of Destinies did not go black. The vermilion glowed. *I forbid it* burned beside his mother's name. The new line on the first page of his own ledger burned too: **At sixteen, took up the brush and altered the ledger.**

And on each of the seven deferral slips, four words lit up one by one: *Deferred for one year.*

The signatures beneath them stayed dark.

“Senior Xie,” the junior clerk whispered. “That's your hand.”

Wujiu did not need to be told. He knew the hook he left at the end of every stroke, the one his teacher had rapped his knuckles for and never broken. All seven slips carried it. The others had only signed. He had written every word.

“Vermilion remembers the hand that holds it,” the keeper said from the dark. “Lamplight lets it forget. I had hoped to keep it forgetting a little longer.”

“I fell into a well at sixteen.”

“You climbed out of one.” The keeper's voice did not move closer. “You came up this mountain soaked to the waist, in the middle of the night, and found your own name already written. You took the red brush from this desk and wrote yourself a year. A mortal who alters the ledger is not allowed to remember it. So I took the memory, and I gave you a clerk's seat, where I could watch your hand.”

“And the signatures?”

“An order needs someone to pay for it. Every year at first frost, someone came up and signed for you. Last year it was Chen Aman. The ledger took her hand first, and the rest of her when the year ran out.”

Wujiu thought of the cloth around her left hand, and of the ointment she had carried up the stairs with the right.

He turned to his mother's ledger. In the vermilion light, the entry had changed. Her name was settled now, and beneath *To die tomorrow* the hour had been filled in: **At the first bell of dawn.** The rejection note had changed too. *Settle the original debt* now ended with three more words: **One brush hand.**

“It doesn't want your thumb,” the keeper said. “It never did. It wants the hand that wrote.”

Wujiu looked down. His right hand was glowing faintly from the fingertips to the wrist, the same color as the ink.

“If I give it,” he said, “she lives?”

“She lives until her own page. And you will never write again. Not a ledger, not a letter, not your name.”

The clerk felt for the flint on the shelf and struck it. The spark showed Wujiu's face for an instant, quite calm.

Before the second spark caught, someone knocked at the Hall's gate below. Three knocks, slow, the way the villagers knocked when they brought offerings.

The clerk went to the window. When he turned back, his voice was very small.

“It's your mother. She's carrying a packet of chilblain ointment.”
